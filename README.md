India’s CSR Expenditure Reflects Growing Corporate Responsibility
The CSR framework in India has established a structured approach for eligible companies to contribute to social development. Under Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013, qualifying companies are required to spend at least 2% of their average net profits from the three immediately preceding financial years on eligible CSR activities, subject to applicable legal provisions.
The reported CSR expenditure of ₹40,794 crore in FY25 demonstrates the significant financial resources that businesses can direct towards social welfare. These investments can support initiatives in education, healthcare, environmental sustainability, rural development, livelihood generation, women’s empowerment, and other eligible areas under Schedule VII of the Companies Act.
Corporate funding can help address gaps in public services, strengthen community institutions, and create opportunities for disadvantaged populations. However, the overall amount spent does not automatically indicate how effectively the funds have reached the communities that need them most. The quality of project selection, implementation, monitoring, and evaluation also plays a major role in determining the long-term value of CSR investments.
Uneven Distribution of CSR Funds Remains a Challenge
One of the key concerns highlighted by the current CSR landscape is the uneven distribution of corporate funding. While some states, districts, and urban centres attract significant CSR investments, other regions continue to face difficulties in securing adequate financial support for development projects.
Several factors can contribute to this imbalance. Corporate headquarters and major industrial facilities are often concentrated in particular locations, influencing where companies identify projects and establish partnerships. The availability of experienced NGOs, implementation agencies, and established development programmes can also affect funding decisions.
In contrast, smaller NGOs and organisations working in remote or underserved areas may struggle to attract corporate partners. Limited administrative capacity, insufficient project documentation, and difficulties in demonstrating measurable outcomes can further restrict their access to CSR funding.
This uneven distribution highlights the importance of needs-based CSR planning. Companies can improve their strategies by identifying communities with significant development gaps, consulting local stakeholders, and evaluating projects according to their potential social impact rather than relying exclusively on established networks or familiar locations.

Importance of Monitoring and Social Impact Assessment
Effective monitoring and evaluation are essential for understanding the outcomes of CSR expenditure. Financial reports can indicate how much money has been allocated or spent, but they may not fully explain whether a programme has improved the lives of its intended beneficiaries.
Social impact assessment helps organisations examine changes resulting from development initiatives. Depending on the project, this may involve measuring school attendance, healthcare access, employment outcomes, income improvements, environmental benefits, or community participation.
Baseline studies can establish the conditions that existed before a programme began, while midline and endline assessments can help track progress over time. Community consultations, beneficiary feedback, field visits, and reliable data collection can provide additional insights into project effectiveness.
These processes also help identify implementation challenges and opportunities for improvement. By using evidence to guide future decisions, companies can refine their CSR strategies, improve resource allocation, and strengthen accountability to stakeholders.
For organisations seeking to maximise the value of CSR funding, the focus should extend beyond expenditure figures to measurable outcomes, long-term sustainability, and the needs of the communities being served.
